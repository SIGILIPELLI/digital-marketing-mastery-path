# 02 · Audiences, Personas & Customer Journeys

Marketing to "everyone" reaches no one. This module shows you how to define exactly who you're talking to, what they care about, and the steps they take before buying — so every message, keyword and ad you create later has a target.

## 1. Segment the market

1. List the groups who could buy from the business. For Chai Corner: nearby office workers, college students, families on weekends, companies ordering catering.
2. Describe each group by **demographics** (age, income, location), **behaviour** (when and how they buy) and **needs** (speed, price, quality, convenience).
3. Score each segment on size, value per customer and how easily you can reach it. Office catering is small in number but high value (₹6,000 per order) and reachable through LinkedIn and Google searches.
4. Choose one **primary** segment to focus on first, and one **secondary**.

## 2. Build a persona

1. Give the persona a name and role: *"Neha, 31, admin manager at a 60-person IT firm in Baner."*
2. **Goals**: arrange reliable snacks for Friday team meetings without hassle.
3. **Pain points**: late deliveries, vendors who don't send GST invoices, food that arrives cold.
4. **Where she looks**: Google ("office snacks catering Baner"), colleague recommendations, Swiggy/Zomato reviews.
5. **Objections**: "Can they handle 60 people? Will they invoice the company properly?"
6. **What convinces her**: photos of past office orders, a clear per-head price, a GST invoice, and a quick WhatsApp response.
7. Base personas on evidence — talk to 5 real customers, read reviews, check what people search — not guesses.

## 3. Map the customer journey

1. **Trigger** — Neha's manager asks for Friday snacks.
2. **Search** — she Googles, sees three cafés and a catering aggregator.
3. **Evaluate** — she opens two websites, checks the menu PDF and reviews.
4. **Contact** — she sends a WhatsApp message to the one that shows a clear "Office catering" button.
5. **Purchase** — confirms 60 portions, pays by UPI, receives a GST invoice.
6. **Experience** — food arrives on time; the team likes it.
7. **Repeat** — the café sends a monthly menu on WhatsApp; she reorders.
8. For each step, note the **touchpoint** (Google, website, WhatsApp) and what could make her drop out. Those drop-off points become your marketing to-do list.

## 4. Write the value proposition

1. Use the format: *For [persona] who [need], [business] is the [category] that [key benefit], unlike [alternative].*
2. Example: *"For office admins who need reliable team snacks, Chai Corner is the local café caterer that delivers hot, GST-invoiced orders for 20–100 people on time — unlike aggregators that can't guarantee timing."*
3. Turn it into a headline (8–12 words) and three supporting points you'll reuse on the website, ads and social posts.

## Cheat sheet

| Tool | Purpose | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Segmentation | Find groups worth targeting | 3–5 segments scored |
| Persona | One realistic buyer | Goals, pains, channels, objections |
| Journey map | Steps before and after purchase | Touchpoints + drop-off risks |
| Value proposition | Why choose you | One sentence + headline |
| Evidence sources | Make personas real | Interviews, reviews, search data |

## How It Actually Works

Ad platforms and search engines reward relevance, and relevance is defined by the audience, not the business. A persona forces every later decision — keywords, ad copy, landing page layout, posting times — to be judged against one specific person's goals and objections. The journey map exposes that most lost sales happen in the middle: people who were interested but hit friction (no price, no reply, no trust signal). Fixing those moments is usually cheaper than buying more traffic.
